*When software grows faster than our mental model of it, productivity can turn into exhaustion, and the profession needs to find its center again.*

![](/assets/image.jpg)

Not long ago, one of the clearest limits on a software project was the number of hands available to write code. There was a backlog, there were deadlines, there was a list of problems that looked simple on paper and took days, sometimes weeks, to become a stable solution.

Now, part of that limit has shifted.

With AI tools capable of suggesting architectures, generating tests, creating components, and filling entire files in seconds, producing code has become much cheaper and faster. That does not mean software development has become simple. It means the hardest work has changed: **understanding what is being built, why it is being built, and what consequences that change creates for the system and for the people who depend on it.**

For many people, this transition does not feel liberating. It feels exhausting.

## The speed that comes at a cost

It is easy to be drawn to the idea of several agents working in parallel. While one creates the interface, another refactors a data layer, a third writes tests, and another investigates an error. From the outside, it looks like a superpower: we can finally do more in less time.

But there is a less comfortable question: who will understand the result of all of that?

Every task completed by an AI carries decisions, assumptions, dependencies, and side effects. Even when the code works, someone still has to assess whether it makes sense in the product's context, respects the system's constraints, and will remain understandable when the next incident happens.

The cost does not disappear. It changes shape.

Constant context switching, between conversations, diffs, plans, logs, reviews, and agent responses, can create a kind of fatigue that is hard to name. You end the day with many files changed and little sense of ownership. There was activity, there was delivery, perhaps even a solid demo. But your mental map of the system fell behind.

That is where something like **cognitive debt** appears.

## When code grows faster than understanding

Technical debt is a familiar idea: shortcuts and poor decisions today make software more expensive to change tomorrow. Cognitive debt is different, although it often travels alongside technical debt.

It appears when the system evolves faster than the shared understanding of it.

The code may be formatted, tested, and in production. Even so, the team may not be able to explain with confidence why a rule exists, which flow is critical, or where an apparently local change can break something important. The software stops being merely complex; it becomes opaque.

AI can intensify this problem because it is excellent at producing plausible answers at scale. It can move through paths very quickly, but it has no real responsibility for the destination. If the initial context is incomplete, an assumption is wrong, or the problem is poorly defined, speed only takes us faster toward a solution we may not have needed to build.

And when the tool enters a cycle of trial and error, the developer still needs to be the person capable of stopping the loop. For that, it is not enough to know how to prompt better. You need a mental model of the system, however imperfect, and the willingness to return to the basic question: “what is happening here?”

## Grieving technical discovery

There is also a more intimate loss in this change.

Many developers fell in love with this profession because of small moments of discovery: finally understanding a `LEFT JOIN`, realizing why an abstraction failed, applying a design pattern in the right place, finding the cause of a bug that seemed impossible. It was not just about productivity. It was an experience of building knowledge, autonomy, and intellectual joy.

When the answer arrives before the investigation, part of that experience can disappear.

That does not mean we should romanticize the pain of spending hours stuck on an error. Nor does it mean that every line of code written manually is proof of value. AI can absolutely eliminate repetitive work and expand our ability to deliver.

But there is a difference between delegating a task and abandoning the relationship with what we are creating.

If we accept code we cannot explain, it is understandable that our sense of authorship fades. The result exists in the repository, but it does not become knowledge within us. And when writing syntax is no longer the main differentiator, an inevitable insecurity appears: if that is not what defines me, then what is my role?

## Code is the path, not the destination

Perhaps the answer is not that developers have become less important. Perhaps the center of the profession is becoming more visible.

Code has always been a means of transforming an intention into something useful: a better experience for someone, a less painful process, a clearer decision, a problem solved. In many teams, though, it was possible to confuse the means with the end because writing code demanded so much effort that it took up almost all the attention.

With AI reducing part of that effort, there is more room and more responsibility for what cannot be easily outsourced:

* understanding the problem deeply before asking for a solution;
* identifying constraints that are not written in any ticket;
* choosing what is worth building and what should stay out;
* noticing technical, human, and product risks;
* creating criteria for deciding whether an answer truly “works”;
* holding a direction when many options are possible.

This is not a devaluation of engineering. It is a more demanding version of it. Technical skill remains essential, but it needs to be connected to judgment, context, and responsibility.

AI can lend us speed. It can even lend a kind of operational intelligence. But it has no conviction. It does not feel the impact of a confusing feature, a poorly planned migration, or a system that is impossible to maintain. It does not bear the cost of a decision after the conversation ends.

We do.

## How to regain control of the pace

There is no universal rule for using AI without burning out, but a few practices can help protect understanding amid the acceleration.

### 1. Default to clarity over parallelism

Multiple agents can be useful for genuinely independent tasks. But parallelizing everything creates a queue of results that someone will need to review and integrate. For complex work, a more sequential flow can be faster in the end because it reduces the mental reconstruction required afterward.

### 2. Ask for plans before implementation

Before generating code, ask for a reading of the problem, alternatives, risks, affected files, and a verifiable plan. A good plan does not remove the need for review, but it turns AI from a machine that dumps changes into a more auditable reasoning partner.

### 3. Require explanations you could defend

An answer is not ready just because it passes a test. Ask the tool to explain the decisions it made, the assumptions it adopted, and the limits of the solution. If the explanation does not make sense to you, it may be too early to accept the change.

### 4. Preserve space to learn, not only to deliver

Not every investigation needs to be outsourced. Sometimes, understanding a problem on your own is the most lasting way to reduce future work. The goal is not to refuse help; it is not to turn every question into a black box.

### 5. Return to the user and the problem

As the volume of code increases, it is easy to lose the connection with why it exists. Simple questions can act as a compass: who benefits from this? What pain are we removing? What would happen if we did not make this change? What signal would show that it worked?

## Between intelligence and conviction

Perhaps this is the most human part of the conversation about AI in software development: we are not only learning new tools. We are renegotiating our relationship with work, learning, and the feeling of being good at what we do.

If you feel more tired even while delivering more, that is not necessarily a lack of adaptability or an inability to keep up. It may be a sign that speed is exceeding your capacity to process, understand, and decide calmly.

We do not have to choose between rejecting AI and handing it the wheel entirely. We can use it as a powerful ship, but we still need to take the helm and consult the compass.

Code may be the path. Understanding is what still allows us to choose where to go.

And at a time when tools produce more and more, perhaps the most valuable skill is preserving the clarity to build only what we truly believe in.
